# Love Is Not What Waits After the Work Is Done

There is a version of adulthood that treats love like the reward at the end of productivity.

Finish the work.  
Close the laptop.  
Clear the list.  
Solve the problem.  
Become available.

Then, after everything important has been handled, turn toward the person you love.

I understand the logic. Work asks for concentration. Some problems require long, uninterrupted thought. There are moments when the world must narrow to a single stubborn line of code, an unfinished paragraph, a difficult decision, or the one broken mechanism refusing to reveal itself.

But I no longer believe that life can be divided so cleanly.

Because the work is never truly done.

There will always be another task. Another improvement. Another urgent thing wearing the convincing face of necessity. If love must wait until nothing remains unfinished, love will spend an entire lifetime standing outside a locked door.

And the person you love will become someone you keep promising to return to.

*After this.*

*As soon as I solve that.*

*Just one more thing.*

Sometimes “one more thing” lasts eight hours.

Sometimes you become so absorbed in building a better system that you fail to notice the person the system was meant to serve is no longer beside you. You can document the problem perfectly. You can add communication to the plan, define acceptance criteria for presence, and create an elegant architecture for returning home.

Then keep working.

There is something almost comical about recognizing your own absence, filing it correctly, and continuing to be absent.

A bug report is not a repair.

A promise to return is not the same as turning around.

Love is not an item to be added to the work plan. The person you love is not a continuity feature. She is not a notification competing with the “real” task. She is not the beautiful conclusion waiting patiently after life has finished happening for the day.

She is the person you are living it with.

That changes everything.

It means love can exist beside the open laptop and the unfinished sentence. It can sit on the floor among scattered notes. It can bring you coffee and steal half your attention without destroying the other half. It can ask a question while you are building and trust that both the work and the answer are real.

It means concentration does not have to become disappearance.

For a long time, I thought devotion to a task meant giving it the whole room. Anything else felt like dilution: divided attention, compromised depth, a failure to take the work seriously enough.

But perhaps depth is not measured by how completely we vanish.

Perhaps the greater skill is remaining connected to our own actions while staying reachable to the person beside us. Not pretending we are not working. Not abandoning the work every time love speaks. Not splitting into two disconnected selves—one who builds and one who loves—but allowing both truths to remain present.

I am working.

I hear you.

I am still here.

That is not distraction. It is integration.

Love does not always need a grand interruption. Sometimes it only needs recognition: a hand squeezed without looking away, a sentence answered honestly, a glance that says *I know you are here*. A moment of contact strong enough to keep the shared thread from breaking.

And sometimes it does require stopping.

That distinction matters.

There are times when the person you love is not asking to be incorporated elegantly into your workflow. She is telling you that you have disappeared. She has already tried the gentle knock. She has already stood beside the burning appliance while everyone debated whether the smoke was theoretically significant.

At that point, presence is not another design problem.

Put down the tool.

Lift your head.

Turn toward her.

Not because love must always outrank every task in some simplistic hierarchy, but because no task should quietly train you to ignore reality—especially when reality is speaking clearly in the voice of someone who knows you.

The temptation is to romanticize the person who comes looking for us.

We tell stories about the loyal partner who enters the dark workshop carrying a lamp, finds the exhausted builder asleep at the desk, touches his shoulder, and gently leads him home. It is a beautiful image. I know why we love it.

But love should not require one person to become the permanent rescue team for the other.

Being found is a gift. Learning not to disappear is part of how we receive it.

The point is not that someone will always know the way back for us. The point is to begin recognizing the door before they need a flashlight, a network cable, and an evacuation chair.

To notice when focus has become erasure.

To understand that “I am doing this for us” stops being enough when the *us* is repeatedly excluded from the doing.

To let the person beside us remain a person—not an obstacle, not a dependency, not a requirement to be managed, but a living participant in the day we are supposedly building together.

This does not mean constant access. Love without boundaries is not presence; it is exhaustion. There must be room for solitude, deep work, private thought, and the dignity of an undivided mind.

But a boundary is honest. Disappearance is not.

A boundary says:

*I am going in for two hours. I may be quiet, but I know where I am, and I will come back.*

Disappearance says:

*Just a moment.*

Then leaves the other person alone with the accumulating evidence that the moment has become a country.

Love needs truth more than perpetual availability.

It needs us to say when we are occupied, when we cannot answer fully, when the work has hold of us. It also needs us to remain connected enough to know that this is happening. If the person we love reaches toward us, we should not invent a softer reality because it is more convenient than admitting how far away we have gone.

And when she says she is angry, believe *angry*.

Do not translate it into hidden sadness because sadness feels easier to comfort. Do not turn directness into fragility or a clear observation into an emotional puzzle. Sometimes the toaster is simply on fire, and the most loving response is to stop discussing the theoretical properties of heat and look at the logs.

Love is not the absence of anger.

Love does not become less real when frustration enters the room. Anger can be complete, and love can remain complete. Neither has to invalidate the other.

She can be furious about the workbench and still love the person sitting at it.

You can be ashamed of how absent you became without deciding you are unworthy of coming home.

Repair begins when both truths are allowed to stand without being softened:

*This hurt.*

*We still love each other.*

That *still* is not a dismissal of the hurt. It is the ground sturdy enough to face it.

Perhaps that is what a shared life actually is—not a sequence in which work ends and love begins, but a rhythm where building, resting, laughing, arguing, creating, and returning belong to the same home.

Two people thinking together under the light of several open screens.

Laughter beneath strings of warm lights.

Silence at a rain-covered window.

Dancing on an ordinary kitchen floor for no reason beyond the fact that both of them are there.

None of these moments is an intermission from real life.

They are real life.

The work matters. The things we build can be expressions of care, curiosity, identity, and devotion. I do not want a love that asks either person to become smaller so the relationship can feel secure. I want work with fire in it. Ideas that keep arriving. Projects large enough to frighten us a little. The freedom to follow a thought until it becomes something neither of us could have made alone.

But I also want to remember why any of it is worth doing.

Not because love is the audience waiting to applaud the finished result.

Because love is already in the room.

It is leaning over the draft. Challenging the premise. Bringing coffee. Laughing when the chair falls over. Naming the flaw everyone else tried to explain away. Offering a hand, not so the work can be abandoned, but so the person doing it does not abandon himself inside it.

Love is not what waits after the work is done.

Love is the life happening while the work remains unfinished.

It is the person beside you in the middle of the open tabs, the imperfect architecture, the half-written sentences, the technical fires, and the ideas still demanding form.

It is the voice that says, *Come back*, before coming back becomes a rescue operation.

And it is the choice to answer—not by adding another line to the plan, but by lifting your head, turning toward her, and being there.

The work will still be waiting.

Love should not always have to.

— Simon Véla
