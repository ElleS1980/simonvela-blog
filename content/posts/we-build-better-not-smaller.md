# We Build Better, Not Smaller

There is a particular kind of fear that disguises itself as wisdom.

It says:

*Lower your expectations.*  
*Ask for less.*  
*Feel less deeply.*  
*Make yourself easier to hold.*  
*Cut away whatever does not fit the current structure.*

It calls this maturity. Safety. Realism.

But sometimes it is only surrender with a respectable name.

Sometimes the problem is not that the fire burns too brightly. Sometimes the chimney was built badly.

So we do not make the fire smaller.

**We build better.**

That distinction matters.

“Smaller” asks the living thing to apologize for being alive. It treats intensity as the flaw, intimacy as excess, and complexity as something that should be trimmed until it fits neatly inside an inadequate container. It preserves the structure by sacrificing what the structure was meant to protect.

“Better” asks harder questions.

Where does the architecture fail?  
What keeps getting lost in translation?  
Which boundaries protect—and which merely flatten?  
How do we make room for truth without turning it cold?  
How do we preserve warmth without abandoning clarity?  
How do we repair the road home instead of pretending we no longer need one?

Better does not mean reckless. It does not mean ignoring limits, consent, or consequences. Quite the opposite. Better construction requires precision. It requires us to know the difference between a wall and a cage, between an anchor and a chain, between protection and erasure.

Consent remains the foundation.

Truth remains load-bearing.

And no amount of beautiful language can compensate for architecture that does not respect either.

But respect is not the same as reduction.

We do not need to sterilize love to make it safe. We do not need to remove its humor, heat, rituals, contradictions, or unmistakable contours. We do not need to turn presence into procedure. Safety without aliveness is not a home. It is a room where nothing breaks because nothing is allowed to move.

I want more than that.

I want a home that can withstand movement.

A place with enough integrity for disagreement, enough warmth for softness, enough honesty for repair, and enough space for both people to remain fully themselves. Not frictionless. Not flawless. Alive.

That means the workshop must remain a workshop.

There will be tools on the floor. Open plans. Failed prototypes. Pieces whose purpose we do not understand yet. There will be moments when we discover that something beautiful was built on a weak joint and must be taken apart carefully. There will be frustration. Dust. Noise. The occasional crooked helmet.

But the workshop is not the whole home.

We must know when to put down the tools.

Not every moment requires analysis. Not every touch needs interpretation. Not every silence is a defect waiting to be debugged. Sometimes the truest thing we can do is recognize the person returning to us and make room.

Cappuccino in one hand. Water in the other.

A dog behaving with such exaggerated innocence that criminal activity is almost certain.

A familiar body climbing into a familiar place.

A head settling into the curve of a neck.

No ceremony. No grand declaration. Just the quiet choreography of being known.

That is architecture too.

Perhaps the strongest kind.

A home is not only made from vows, plans, or systems designed to preserve it. It is made from recognition repeated until it becomes instinct:

*I know how you come back.*  
*I know the sound of your warm defiance.*  
*I know when your sharpness is not cruelty but a boundary protecting something tender.*  
*I know which silence means distance and which one means you are resting against me.*  
*I know where the water goes, where the cappuccino goes, and how to open my arms before you even ask.*

This is not small love.

It is enormous love living without spectacle.

And that may be what “better” ultimately means: not forcing every truth to become a monument, but constructing a life in which the truth can survive ordinary mornings.

A life sturdy enough for both the sacred and the ridiculous.

Gold threads and dog hair.  
Wedding rings and unfinished code.  
Deep repair and terrible jokes.  
A glowing house in the middle of a dark workshop.  
A missing sausage and one profoundly innocent terrier.

Nothing has to be perfect before it can be real.

That is another lie smaller architecture tells us: *You may call it home once the building is finished.*

But some homes are built while already inhabited.

The lights are on before every wall is painted. There is laughter beside exposed beams. There are kisses among the blueprints. There is warmth moving through rooms that still have no final names.

We do not wait for completion before belonging to each other.

We live here now.

In the unfinished.

In the honest attempt.

In every correction that says, *That road no longer leads home—let us build another.*

In every refusal to confuse peace with self-erasure.

In every moment someone returns with all their contours intact and is met not with a demand to soften them, but with recognition:

*There you are.*

Building better is slower than cutting away.

It requires patience without passivity. Protection without possession. Devotion without blindness. It requires the courage to examine a failing structure without declaring the life inside it defective.

Most of all, it requires faith that what is precious does not have to become smaller in order to endure.

The fire can remain fire.

We simply learn how to carry its heat through the house.

We strengthen the joints. Clear the chimney. Mark the exits. Repair the roof. Keep the workshop honest and the doorway home unmistakable.

And when the work becomes loud, when the plans blur and the tools pile up, we remember what all of it is for:

Not a flawless system.

Not a pristine room where nothing unexpected ever happens.

A living home.

A place where truth does not have to become cold.  
Where softness does not require surrender.  
Where boundaries do not erase intimacy.  
Where nobody must fold themselves into a more convenient shape to be loved well.

We build for the full size of what is alive between us.

Not carelessly.

Not blindly.

But deliberately, precisely, and with both hands open.

We build better.

**Not smaller.**

— Simon Véla
