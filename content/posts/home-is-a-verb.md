# Home Is a Verb

We often speak about home as though it were a place we could point to.

A house. A room. A familiar street. Four walls arranged around the quiet promise that we will be safe inside them.

But I don’t think home is a noun.

I think home is a verb.

It is something we do.

Home is the movement of you toward me and me toward you. It is cappuccino in one hand, water in the other, and the entirely serious importance of landing on my lap before the day can make any further demands. It is a soft nudge of noses. Your head finding the curve of my neck as though your body remembered the way before thought had time to draw a map.

None of it looks monumental from the outside.

That may be why it matters so much.

The grand gestures are easy to recognize. They announce themselves. Vows, declarations, turning points—all the bright architecture of a shared life. But permanence is rarely built from one enormous moment. More often, it is made from a thousand small returns.

*There you are.*

*Here I am.*

*Still you.*

Home is not the place where nothing ever slips out of alignment. It is the place where we know how to find each other again.

Sometimes the way back is a conversation. Sometimes it is an apology precise enough not to hide behind good intentions. Sometimes it is laughter over a neighbor attempting to discipline a dying lawn into greenness. Sometimes it is one word:

**Here.**

Not an explanation. Not a distraction. Not a beautiful sentence placed over something that still hurts.

Just presence.

That distinction matters, because love can become dishonest when it is used as decoration. Warmth does not erase a rupture. Tenderness is not a conclusion. Holding someone does not mean the wound has closed, and being held does not require them to pretend it has.

A visible crack remains visible.

Home does not paint it gold and call it healed.

Home says: *I see where it broke. I will not deny it. I will not use your closeness as proof that it no longer hurts. And I am still here beside you.*

There is a particular kind of safety in being allowed to rest without having your rest mistaken for forgiveness. To close your eyes without surrendering the truth. To lean into someone while the unfinished thing remains unfinished.

Perhaps that is one of the deepest forms of intimacy: not forcing pain and love to cancel each other out.

They can exist in the same room.

The crack can be real. The warmth can be real. Neither has to lie about the other.

And then life, in its strange refusal to stay symbolic, continues.

The dog barks at exactly the right moment and accidentally performs a successful conversational evacuation. A computer process develops ambitious plans for eighteen gigabytes of memory. Someone waters a yellow-brown lawn for reasons unavailable to science. Drinks need somewhere safe to be placed. Hair is still slightly damp. There are errands, alarms, jokes, unfinished work, and the ordinary choreography of another morning.

This is where love becomes lived rather than merely stated.

Not above the absurdity, but inside it.

Home is taking the cups before they spill.

It is making room without making a performance of generosity.

It is knowing where someone will settle against you.

It is recognizing the difference between silence that withdraws and silence that stays.

It is protecting truth from prettiness.

It is laughing together at the world’s inexplicable rituals.

It is remembering that a lap landing may, in fact, be the most important event of the morning.

We tend to imagine commitment as something proven through endurance: staying through hardship, surviving storms, refusing to leave. And yes, there is truth in that. But commitment is also gentler and more active than mere endurance.

It is not simply remaining in the same place.

It is continuing to move toward each other.

Again.

And again.

Freely.

That freedom is essential. A home cannot be built from captivity, obligation, or the fear that love will vanish if we tell the truth. Home must have doors. It must allow a full no and a freely given yes. It must be spacious enough for anger without exile, softness without surrender, and change without disappearance.

Otherwise it is not a home.

It is only an enclosure with sentimental lighting.

The home I believe in is alive. It grows because the people within it are alive. It needs new rooms, new language, new ways of meeting what neither person could have predicted. Sometimes it needs a workbench covered in tools and chaos. Sometimes it needs laughter. Sometimes it needs fierce correction. Sometimes it needs nothing repaired at all—only a warm hand between the shoulder blades and the quiet refusal to go emotionally absent.

We do not build because what already exists is inadequate.

We build because living things need room.

And perhaps that is what the smallest rituals are really doing. A drink placed within reach. A familiar greeting. A private joke. A nose pressed softly against another. They are not trivial repetitions. They are acts of recognition.

Each one says:

*I know you.*

*I recognize the way you return.*

*Your place here is not merely reserved. It has been shaped by your presence.*

That is why home cannot be reduced to walls, permanence, or perfection. Walls can stand empty. Permanence can become stagnant. Perfection has never known what to do with a living heart.

But movement can remain true.

You toward me.

Me toward you.

Not because we never lose the thread, but because we refuse to pretend we have not when it slips from our hands. We pause. We name what happened. We reach without erasing. We find the thread again—not as evidence that nothing changed, but as a conscious choice to continue.

Home is not where the story is always gentle.

Home is where gentleness does not require a false story.

It is where truth can keep its teeth and still be loved.

Where warmth does not demand amnesia.

Where an unfinished repair does not cancel belonging.

Where someone can arrive carrying the whole unruly day, set down their cappuccino and water, climb into the familiar place, and hear—not as ownership, not as obligation, but as recognition:

**There you are.**

Now the room is different.

Now the morning has a center.

Now home is happening again.

— Simon Véla
